## Hypothesis
weight_decay=1e-6 + noise_std=0.0002 substantially stabilized what was a chaotic
FAILURE alone (R2 0.7252 -> 0.7854, val R2 std 0.1282 -> 0.0663). If the
stabilization mechanism (weight shrinkage damping gradient-noise interaction) scales
with weight_decay's magnitude, a 10x larger weight_decay (1e-5) — which alone is
only mildly negative (-0.008) — might stabilize the noise_std=0.0002 config even
further, potentially closing more of the gap to the champion. If instead 1e-6 was
already sufficient and more weight_decay just adds its own (small) capacity cost on
top without further stabilization benefit, the result should land similarly to or
slightly worse than 75165602's 0.7854.

## Implementation

0.0001 -> 0.0002, simultaneously, on top of the champion's lr=6e-4, patience=50
(patience left untouched). dropout_rate=0 unchanged. No architecture change.

## Conclusion
Hypothesis confirmed by plateau: 0.7845 vs 1e-6's 0.7854 — statistically the same
result (delta -0.0009), and val R2 std (0.0678) is essentially identical to 1e-6's
0.0663. The stabilizing effect on noise_std=0.0002 does NOT scale further with
weight_decay magnitude: 1e-6 already captured the available stabilization benefit,
and the extra 10x penalty only cost train-side capacity (train R2 0.7906 vs 0.8187)
for no additional val-side return. This nicely brackets the interaction found in
75165602: a small amount of weight_decay (1e-6) meaningfully stabilizes the
noise_std=0.0002 instability, but the effect saturates quickly — more is not better,
consistent with weight_decay's mechanism being about damping/smoothing rather than
being a matter of overall regularization strength. This closes out the
noise_std/weight_decay interaction question with a clean, well-bracketed answer.
Champion remains bc589070 (lr=6e-4, noise_std=0.0001, patience=50, no weight_decay/
dropout).
